Understanding Digital Multimeter Accuracy: ±0.5% vs ±1% — Does It Matter?

👤 CHINYOO Engineering Team 📅 July 9, 2026 🕑 13 min read 📖 Technical Guide

Why "±0.5% Accuracy" Doesn't Mean What You Think

When comparing digital multimeters, you'll see accuracy specifications like ±0.5% of reading + 2 digits or ±1% of reading + 5 digits printed on the box and listed in datasheets. These figures are the closest thing to a universal quality marker for a DMM — a higher accuracy number supposedly means a better, more expensive meter.

But what does "±0.5% of reading" actually mean in practice? Is it worth paying twice as much for a ±0.5% meter over a ±1% meter? And why do some 6000-count meters outperform 19999-count meters on accuracy — even though the higher-count meter looks more impressive on paper?

This guide demystifies multimeter accuracy specifications so you can make an informed buying decision and use your meter with correct expectations of its real-world performance.

What ±0.5% or ±1% Actually Means: The Full Specification Formula

Multimeter accuracy is never expressed as a single percentage. It is always a composite of two terms:

Full Accuracy Formula: ±(% of reading) + ±(number of least significant digits)

Example: A meter rated ±0.5% + 2 digits means both terms apply — you add them, not choose between them.

The % of reading term accounts for proportional errors (gain errors in the A/D converter, resistor tolerance). The digits term (also called count error or quantization error) accounts for the inherent rounding that occurs when a continuous analog voltage is converted to a discrete digital number. The least significant digit (LSD) represents the smallest increment the display can show on the active range.

Worked Example 1: ±0.5% + 2 Digits Meter Measuring 12.00 V

TermCalculationError Contribution
% of reading (0.5%)12.00 V × 0.005±0.060 V
Digits (±2 LSD)2 × 0.01 V (on 20 V range)±0.02 V
Total worst-case error0.060 + 0.020±0.080 V
Actual reading band12.00 V ± 0.080 V11.920 V to 12.080 V
Relative accuracy0.080 / 12.00 × 100%±0.67%

Worked Example 2: ±1% + 5 Digits Meter Measuring 12.00 V (Same Meter, Different Spec)

TermCalculationError Contribution
% of reading (1%)12.00 V × 0.01±0.120 V
Digits (±5 LSD)5 × 0.01 V (on 20 V range)±0.05 V
Total worst-case error0.120 + 0.050±0.170 V
Actual reading band12.00 V ± 0.170 V11.830 V to 12.170 V
Relative accuracy0.170 / 12.00 × 100%±1.42%

⚠ Key insight: The digits term becomes more significant when measuring small values on high ranges, and less significant when measuring large values. At 120 V AC, the ±2 digit error on a 600 V range (resolution = 0.1 V) contributes only ±0.2 V to a 120 V reading — a negligible 0.17%. But at 0.12 V on the same range, the same ±0.2 V error becomes a catastrophic ±167% error.

Display Counts and Resolution Explained

The term "count" (or display count) refers to the maximum number that a DMM's display can show before the next higher range is needed. It is the primary determinant of the meter's resolution — the smallest change in the measured quantity that the meter can detect and display.

The Three Common Count Classes

Count ClassMax DisplayResolution (on 20V range)Typical Use Case
2000 counts 1999 0.01 V (10 mV) Basic home/hobby use, outlets, batteries. Lowest cost.
4000 counts 3999 0.001 V (1 mV) on 4 V range Entry-level professional. Better low-voltage resolution than 2000-count.
6000 counts 5999 0.001 V (1 mV) on 6 V range Professional standard. Excellent for automotive, HVAC, general trade.
19999 counts 19999 0.001 V (1 mV) on 20 V range Mid-high end. True 4-digit display (not a half-digit as below).
40000 / 50000 counts 39999 / 49999 0.1 mV on low voltage ranges Precision / lab-grade portable meters.

What Is a "Half-Digit"?

You may see a meter described as a "3½-digit" or "3½ digit" meter. The "½" refers to the most significant digit, which can only display 0 or 1 (it cannot display 2–9). A 3½-digit meter with a maximum of 1999 is therefore a 2000-count meter. Similarly, a 3¾-digit meter (max 3999) is a 4000-count meter. The terminology is largely historical but still appears in datasheets.

Why More Counts Does Not Mean More Accuracy

This is the most common misconception in multimeter purchasing. A 19999-count meter can display more digits than a 6000-count meter, but that does not automatically make it more accurate. Here is why:

1. Accuracy and Resolution Are Different Things

Resolution is how fine the meter's display increments are. Accuracy is how close those displayed values are to the true value. A meter can display 19,999 counts (resolution to 0.0001 V on the 20 V range) but still have an accuracy of only ±2% — meaning the displayed 1.0000 V could actually be anywhere from 0.9800 V to 1.0200 V. The extra digits beyond the ±2% uncertainty are simply displaying noise, not information.

Rule of thumb: Your meter's accuracy must be at least 10× better than the tolerance of the component or circuit you're testing. If you're checking a 5% tolerance resistor, a ±0.5% meter is more than adequate. A ±2% meter is already pushing it for 1% tolerance components.

2. A/D Converter Linearity Error

Even if the A/D converter is perfectly calibrated at one voltage, it may have integral non-linearity (INL) errors across the range — meaning its accuracy degrades in the middle of the range even if it's accurate at the endpoints. High-count meters from reputable manufacturers are designed to minimize INL through tighter calibration and better component matching. A cheap high-count meter may have excellent resolution but poor linearity.

3. Temperature Coefficient

The accuracy specification is only valid at the meter's reference temperature (typically 23 ±5°C or 18–28°C). As temperature changes, the meter's accuracy drifts. Precision meters specify a temperature coefficient (e.g., ±0.05% per °C outside the reference range). A budget 19999-count meter may have a large temperature coefficient that destroys its theoretical precision in field conditions.

The required accuracy depends entirely on what you're measuring and what decisions you need to make from the reading. Here is a practical guide:

Conclusion: Matching Accuracy to Your Real Needs

For the vast majority of field and bench work — testing outlets, checking batteries, troubleshooting HVAC circuits, verifying automotive sensors — a 6000-count ±0.5% True RMS digital multimeter delivers more than enough precision. The extra resolution of a 19999-count meter rarely translates into practical benefits unless you're working with low-level DC signals, precision electronics, or making calibration-verification measurements.

When choosing a meter, prioritize: (1) the correct CAT rating for your work environment, (2) True RMS if you work with non-linear loads, (3) an adequate count class for your lowest expected measurement, and only (4) tighter ±% accuracy if your application genuinely requires it.

Remember: a meter's accuracy specification is a guarantee of worst-case performance under reference conditions. Real-world accuracy may be better — but it will never be worse than the spec sheet claims. Know your meter's limits, and you'll never be surprised by a reading.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a ±0.5% DMM worth the extra cost over a ±1% DMM?
For most professional applications, yes. The ±0.5% meter provides a meaningful accuracy improvement — about 2× better in many measurement scenarios. However, if your work only involves checking mains voltage at outlets (220–240 V), the ±1% meter is adequate: a ±1% error on 240 V is ±2.4 V, which is imperceptible for most electrical work. The ±0.5% advantage becomes critical when measuring low voltages (under 10 V), working with precision components, or calculating power/energy efficiency where small percentage errors compound.
What's the difference between 6000 counts and 19999 counts? Is more always better?
The count determines display resolution, not accuracy. A 19999-count meter on a 20 V range shows 1 mV resolution (0.001 V), while a 6000-count meter on the same range shows 0.01 V (10 mV). However, if the 19999-count meter's accuracy specification is ±2% and the 6000-count meter's is ±0.5%, the 6000-count meter will actually produce more reliable readings — its extra digits are meaningful. Always read the accuracy spec before being impressed by a high count number.
What does "+ 2 digits" mean in a multimeter accuracy spec?
The "digits" term (also called count error or LSD error) is an additional fixed error that accounts for the rounding and quantization that occurs when the A/D converter converts the analog signal to a digital number. If a meter's spec is ±0.5% + 2 digits and you're on the 20 V range (resolution = 0.01 V), the digit error contributes ±0.02 V on top of the ±0.5% proportional error. This term is most significant when measuring small values — at 0.5 V, the ±0.02 V digit error becomes a 4% potential error, swamping the ±0.5% RDG specification.
Why do meter's accuracy specs differ between DC and AC?
DC voltage measurement uses a direct A/D conversion path with no waveform processing, so DC accuracy tends to be better (e.g., ±0.5% DC). AC measurement requires either average-sensing (cheaper, only accurate for sine waves) or True RMS computation (more complex, slightly wider tolerance, e.g., ±1.5% AC True RMS). Additionally, AC accuracy is measured with a pure 50/60 Hz sine wave at the meter's reference frequency — readings at other frequencies (e.g., from a VFD at 400 Hz) may have additional frequency response errors not captured in the basic AC accuracy spec.
How often should a digital multimeter be professionally calibrated?
For trade and professional use, annual calibration is recommended by most standards bodies (ISO, NIST guidelines). For quality control and laboratory use, calibration intervals of 6 months or quarterly are common. For hobbyist and occasional professional use, verify your meter's accuracy against a known reference source (calibrated voltage standard, certified reference meter) every 1–2 years. Many ISO 9001 and IEC 61010-based quality systems require documented calibration records with traceable standards — check your industry requirements.
